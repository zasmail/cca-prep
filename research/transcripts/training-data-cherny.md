## Episode description (from platform listings)
Boris Cherny, creator of Claude Code at Anthropic, joins Sequoia partner Lauren Reeder at AI
Ascent 2026 to talk about where coding goes from here — why he hasn't written a line of code in
2026, why he now ships dozens of PRs a day from his phone, and why he believes coding is
effectively solved, at least for the code he writes. Also covered: why loops are the future, why
he thinks Claude Code itself may be 100 lines of code a year from now, and why the invention of
the printing press is the right analogy for what's about to happen to software.

## Themes elaborated in secondary coverage

### The printing press analogy
Cherny compares the coming transformation of software to the aftermath of Gutenberg's press:
before printing, literacy was a specialized scribal profession (~10% of Europeans literate). In
the ~50 years after the press, more literature was published in Europe than in the previous
thousand years, and book costs fell roughly 100x; literacy climbed toward ~70% globally over the
following centuries. His point: coding is moving from a scribe-like specialist skill to a
general-purpose literacy, on a similar trajectory. As reading/writing spread, scribes "spent less
time copying books by hand, which allowed them to spend time doing things they were more
interested in, like bookbinding or drawing art in books" — his analogy for what engineers will do
once coding itself is commoditized.

### On his own workflow
Cherny says he hasn't personally hand-edited code since November (2025), reviewing for
correctness/safety but not writing it, and ships "dozens of PRs a day" from his phone. This has
freed him from "tedious work" to focus on "figuring out what to build" and "talking to users."

### On engineers/titles
He expects that "by the end of the year, everyone is going to be a product manager, and everyone
codes," and that the title "software engineer" starts to give way to "builder" — a transition he
acknowledges "is going to be painful for a lot of people." Engineer value shifts from "whether
they can write code to whether they can break down tasks, clearly articulate goals, validate
outcomes, and manage agents."

### On loops
Parallelizing work via sub-agents/loops/batch currently requires manual user prompting and
product-side tweaks; Cherny frames this as temporary. He notes Claude 4.7 already spontaneously
suggests starting a loop when it notices data changing over time, and can push results to Slack
via MCP — a preview of more autonomous, self-initiated agent loops.

### On Claude Code's own future size
He speculates Claude Code itself could shrink to roughly 100 lines of code within a year, as more
of its capability is "unhobbled" into the model itself rather than hand-built product surface.

### Other judgments attributed to Cherny from the same conference/press cycle (KuCoin recap of "7 key insights")
1. Code is no longer scarce — engineer value shifts to task decomposition, goal articulation,
   outcome validation, and agent management.
2. Software democratizes like the printing press — coding becomes a general skill within
   decades.
3. Product sense and domain expertise become the differentiator once AI levels execution.
4. SaaS moats erode as AI can now handle data migration/process reconstruction "in just a few
   days" rather than months, weakening switching-cost lock-in.
5. Best time for small teams/entrepreneurs — the gap between agile small teams and legacy-drag
   large corporations widens as AI strengthens.
6. MCP persists as the "software glue layer" — described as functioning like two protocols in
   one: an interface for humans and an interface for models.
7. Computer Use remains important for legacy systems/enterprises lacking APIs or MCP support.

### Advice for adaptation
Cherny recommends people "experiment with the tools, get to know them, don't be scared of them,"
and become generalists spanning multiple disciplines rather than staying narrowly technical.
